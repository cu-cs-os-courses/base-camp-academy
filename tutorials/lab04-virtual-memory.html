Watch the kernel page, then be the pager

Part 1 measures the real kernel handing out memory lazily, one page fault at a time. Part 2 puts you inside a paging simulator: every tick it calls your pageit(), and your eviction policy decides how much time 20 processes spend waiting for the disk. This page says what each piece must do, which questions your LRU has to answer each tick, and how to read the simulator's score. It never shows code for the lab files. The README, check/sim/simulator.h and the file headers are the specification; L15–L16 are the lectures behind it.

Step 1

touch.c: watch pages materialize

Goal: print exactly RSS_KB_BEFORE=, RSS_KB_AFTER= and MINFLT_DELTA=, measured around a loop that touches every page of a 64 MiB region.

How to check it yourself (with huge pages off; see Step 2):

echo never > /sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/enabled/tmp/touch# BEFORE: a few thousand kB. AFTER: about 65,536 kB more. DELTA: about 16,384.
What the symptom tells you

A value of -1: that reader failed to open or parse; print the line it found. MINFLT_DELTA about 16,384 but RSS barely grows: your touch reads instead of writing. Reading an untouched page does not give the process a page of its own; why not is a good sentence for report question 1. MINFLT_DELTA of only a few hundred: huge pages are still on (Step 2). RSS_KB_BEFORE already about 65,000: something touched the region before the first measurement.

Step 2

Transparent huge pages: two true answers

No code. Measure both settings and keep both sets of numbers for the report.

cat /sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/enabled # the default; brackets mark the active value/tmp/touch # run 1: huge pages as bootedecho never > /sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/enabled/tmp/touch # run 2: one fault per 4 KiB page
  • Compare the two MINFLT_DELTA values. Their ratio should be close to the number of 4 KiB pages in one 2 MiB page. Compute that number and check.
  • Compare the two RSS growths. Are they different?
  • The setting is not saved: every boot starts from the default. make check turns it off itself before running touch.
Step 3

Meet the simulator

Before writing a policy, get the baseline score and learn what your function can see and do.

gcc -Wall -Werror -O2 -Icheck/sim -o /tmp/test-basic \ check/sim/simulator.c check/sim/programs.c check/sim/pager-basic.c/tmp/test-basic -seed 1 2>&1 | grep ratio # ratio blocked/compute=… (about 15)/tmp/test-basic -help # the other flags

Step 4

Design your LRU

Goal: pageit() keeps every active process's needed page in memory as often as it can, and when it must evict, it evicts the least recently used page.

Each call is one tick. On every tick your function has four jobs. Decide how you will do each one before you write it:

jobthe question you must answer
1 · record useWhich (process, page) pairs are in use on this tick? The skeleton's timestamps array and tick counter exist to store "when was this page last used".
2 · requestFor each active process whose needed page is not in memory: ask for it.
3 · make roomIf the request cannot start, choose a victim: the page with the oldest timestamp, among which pages? Only this process's (local), or all processes' (global)? This is your design decision, and report question 2 asks you to defend it. Never choose a page someone needs right now.
4 · in flightA swap takes 100 ticks, and the page map cannot tell "going out" from "coming in" from "out". What stops your function from choosing the same victim again on the next tick? Does it matter?
Before you write it

Delete the three placeholder lines in the skeleton (the fprintf and exit), and keep tick++ as the last thing the function does on every path. A return in the middle that skips it freezes your clock.

Step 5

Measure and iterate

gcc -Wall -Werror -O2 -Icheck/sim -o /tmp/test-lru \ check/sim/simulator.c check/sim/programs.c submit/pager-lru.cfor s in 1 2 3; do /tmp/test-lru -seed $s 2>&1 | grep ratio; done # make check averages these threefor i in 1 2; do /tmp/test-lru -seed 1 2>&1 | grep ratio; done # must be identical

Use the README's table to see where your average stands. The pass bar is an average below 1.28; an honest LRU usually lands far below it. Only the score matters to the check, so once you pass, the local vs global choice is yours to explore: try both and report what you saw.

What the symptom tells you

"pager-lru not yet implemented": the placeholder lines are still there. The run never ends: rerun with -dead. If it prints "All needed pages swapped out!", then at some tick every process was waiting and nothing was coming in, and the simulator will wait forever: your function evicted without requesting, or requested nothing. Stop it with Ctrl-C. Two runs with the same seed differ: the policy uses something that is not repeatable, such as rand(), the time, or an uninitialized variable. A score around 5 or more: eviction is barely working. Check that job 1 really updates the timestamps, and that job 3 picks the smallest one. It is merely slow: make check gives each run 120 seconds. Look for a loop inside pageit that keeps searching when no victim exists.

Seeing your policy think

-block logs every block and unblock with its tick, and -page every pagein and pageout: /tmp/test-lru -seed 1 -block -page 2>&1 | head -80. Follow one process: how long does it wait for each page, and is it waiting for the same page over and over? The second pattern means you are evicting pages that come back soon. (-csv writes the same events to output.csv and pages.csv in the current directory, for plotting.)

Step 7

Report and submit

Four questions in submit/report.md. How to investigate, not the answers:

  1. The lie of malloc. Paste your Step 1 and Step 2 numbers. For "what did each fault do", compare RSS just before and just after the loop, and think about the read-vs-write experiment. For "major", ask where the data of a page would have to come from for a fault to need the disk.
  2. The 75×. Put your three seeds next to pager-basic's. Explain the gap from what pager-basic refuses to do (Step 3), then describe your victim choice and your local or global decision, with the evidence from Step 5.
  3. Is 100:1 honest? Compute the real ratio from the question's two numbers yourself, and compare it with 100. Then convert: how many instructions could the CPU have run during one fault?
  4. Where LRU is exactly wrong. Pick a loop from the five program shapes, or make one up. Give LRU fewer frames than the loop has pages, and simulate by hand, tick by tick, which page LRU evicts and which page the loop needs next.

Stretch, ungraded

The README's pager-predict.c prefetches from the program shapes. Start from your LRU, and first measure how often a process blocks on a page it has used before. That number bounds how much any predictor can win.
