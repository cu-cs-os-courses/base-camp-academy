Three ways to keep bits,
three ways to lose time

Storage outlives power, and every kind of storage pays for that in its own currency. A magnetic disk pays in milliseconds of mechanical motion, so the OS reorders requests to move the head less. Flash pays in erases, and every cell wears out, so writes must be spread around. A single disk of either kind can fail outright, so RAID spends extra disks to buy speed, safety, or both.

Chapter 0

Magnetic disks

How a disk stores bits, and why reading them takes milliseconds.

0.1Magnetic media

  • A strong enough magnetic field sets a small region of a magnetic film to 1 or 0. The region keeps its magnetic sense even when the power is removed.
  • To read a sequence of bits, either move the head over the media, or move (rotate) the media under a static head. Rotating the media is the easier approach.
  • The disk supports both random and sequential access. Stacking more platters adds capacity at a marginal cost in space and extra heads.

0.2Anatomy of a disk

  • Platter: a spinning disk coated with magnetic film, usually both sides.
  • Read/write head on an arm; all arms move together.
  • Track: one ring on one surface.
  • Sector: a slice of a track, the unit of transfer.
  • Cylinder: the same track on every surface. The heads reach a whole cylinder without moving, which is why schedulers count cylinders.

0.3Time to access data

Three things happen, one after another:

  1. move the head to the right track: the seek time, typically 5–10 ms;
  2. wait for the sector to rotate under the head: the rotational latency, typically 1–5 ms. At 10,000 RPM, half a revolution on average takes 0.5 / (10000/60) s ≈ 3 ms;
  3. read the sector into memory: the transfer time, typically 10–100 µs. At 1 Gb/s, 10 KB is 80,000 bits / 109 bits/s = 0.08 ms.
total delay = seek time + rotational latency + transfer time

Chapter 1

Disk scheduling

Seek time dominates. When several requests are waiting, the order in which the OS serves them decides how far the head travels.

1.1Why schedule the disk?

  • Any technique that reduces seek time or rotational latency is a big win for disk access time.
  • Disk operations take time. At any moment several requests for different places on the disk are waiting in the request queue (Lecture 18's queue, in front of a busy device).
  • The OS can choose the order in which to serve them. Our goal: an algorithm that minimizes seek time.
The running example

Requests arrive for cylinders 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67, in that order. The head starts at cylinder 53; cylinders run from 0 to 200. We compare algorithms by the total number of cylinders the head crosses.

1.2FCFS and SSTF

FCFS serves requests in arrival order: |53−98| + |98−183| + |183−37| + … = 640 cylinders. Easy to implement, but slow: the head would move less if 37 and 14 were served together, and 122 and 124.

SSTF (shortest seek time first) always picks the pending request closest to the head: 65, 67, 37 (closer than 98), 14, 98, 122, 124, 183, for 236 cylinders.

SSTF is only locally optimal: going 53 → 37 → 14 first, then up to 183, crosses 208. And it can starve far requests: while the head sits near 37, a stream of new requests for 39, 35, 38… keeps it there.

1.3OPT

OPT minimizes the back-and-forth: move to the closer edge request, then sweep once to the other end.

53 → 37 → 14 → 65 → 67 → 98 → 122 → 124 → 183: 39 down + 169 up = 208 cylinders. OPT ignores the head's current direction and changes it when that is shorter.

The problem: OPT must be recomputed every time a new request arrives. Its best practical approximation is to scan in one direction, then reverse.

1.4SCAN and C-SCAN

SCAN (the elevator) sweeps in one direction to the edge of the disk, then reverses: 53 → 65 → … → 183 → 200 → 37 → 14, for 333 cylinders. It approximates OPT, never starves anyone, handles new arrivals, and is simple.

But it only approximates OPT, travels to the edge even when no request is there, and is unfair to the edges: after two full sweeps, middle tracks have been served twice and edge tracks once.

C-SCAN (circular SCAN) treats the disk as a circular queue: at the edge, return all the way to the other edge without serving anything, and keep scanning in the same direction. 53 → … → 183 → 200, return to 0, → 14 → 37: 384 cylinders, counting the 200-cylinder return. Service times are fairer.

1.5LOOK and C-LOOK

LOOK does not travel to the edge: it goes only as far as the furthest request in the current direction, then reverses. 53 → … → 183 → 37 → 14: 130 up + 169 down = 299 cylinders. No starvation either.

C-LOOK makes LOOK fairer: from the furthest request in this direction, jump straight back to the furthest request at the other end and keep scanning the same way. 53 → … → 183 → 14 → 37: 130 up + 169 back + 23 up = 322 cylinders.

1.7Disk scheduling in practice

  • In the examples, SCAN, LOOK and C-LOOK would cross far fewer cylinders if the head had started moving down. Try it in §1.6.
  • But the scheduler cannot keep switching direction: that brings back starvation and unfairness. Keeping the scan's direction is important for fairness, at some cost in performance.
  • SSTF or LOOK are reasonable defaults. Today these seek-reducing algorithms usually run inside the disk controller, together with algorithms that reduce rotational latency.

1.8Disk scheduling vs. file layout

  • How blocks are allocated affects access time. A file spread across the disk increases seeks; contiguous allocation minimizes them. Choose free blocks near the file's other data.
  • Opening a file for the first time means finding the directory, the file's header, and its data, which may be spread across the disk: large seeks.
  • Put directories in the middle tracks, so at most half the disk is crossed to reach a file; put file headers near their data, or near their directory. Lecture 20 builds file systems that do this.

Chapter 2

Flash memory

No moving parts, so no seeks. The new costs are erasing and wear.

2.1What is flash memory?

Flash is solid-state storage: bits live in an electronic chip, not on a magnetic disk, and are programmed and erased electrically, with no mechanical parts. It is non-volatile: permanent, with caveats. It is in SSDs, memory cards, USB drives, phones and cameras.

Flash is a form of EEPROM (electrically erasable programmable read-only memory): bits can be rewritten again and again with ordinary electrical signals. Its name comes from erasing a large block of memory quickly and cheaply, like a flash of light.

RAMmain memory; volatile
ROMburned in at the factory; read only
PROMwritten once, with a special machine
EPROMrewritten many times, with a special machine
EEPROM · flashrewritten with ordinary electrical signals; non-volatile

2.2Flash vs. disk

Advantages
  • much faster access (lower latency);
  • survives kinetic shock, pressure, water;
  • more compact and lighter;
  • lower power, typically a third to a half of a disk's.
Disadvantages
  • more costly per byte;
  • limited lifetime;
  • erases are costly.

2.3Cells, NAND and NOR

Each bit is a floating-gate transistor. Charge trapped on the floating gate shifts the voltage at which the transistor conducts. To read a bit, apply a read voltage to the control gate and measure the current: current flows → "1"; no current → "0". Cells sit in a matrix of control-gate lines ("words") and drain lines ("bits"), wired in one of two ways:

NAND flashNOR flash
cellsmaller, fewer contacts: cheaper, denserlarger
accessby word: 100–1000 bits at a time; slower random byte accessby byte; faster random access
speedslower read (~1 µs), faster write (~1 µs); erase ~2 ms; ~5 MB/s sustained writesfast read (~100 ns), slow write (~10 µs); erase ~750 ms; ~0.2 MB/s sustained writes
used fordata: secondary storage that reads in chunkscode: ROM-like program storage, read byte by byte

The original iPhone used both: multi-GB NAND for media files and multi-MB NOR for code.

Numbers age fast

These are classic textbook figures. Modern NAND reads and programs a whole page (4–16 KB) at a time, and SSD controllers hide the details behind a disk-like interface.

2.4How flash is organized

Flash is divided into sectors (also called erase blocks), each holding many pages. Example: 64 pages per sector, 16 sectors, 4 KB per page → 4 MB of flash.

The last 12–16 bytes of each page are reserved for error detection and correction (ECC) and bookkeeping; the flash file system can keep its own information there.

2.5Writes are easy, rewrites are costly

  • Reads and writes happen below page granularity: a byte (NOR) or a word (NAND), named by page + offset.
  • But a write proceeds immediately only if that memory has been erased and not yet written. Rewriting memory that already holds data needs an erase first.
  • And erasing happens only a whole sector at a time. To change one byte that was already written, erase the entire sector containing it, then write the byte. That is the price of cheap "flash" technology.
  • Rewrites happen all the time. Deleting a file frees its pages, but before they can be given to another file, their sectors must be erased. On a magnetic disk a rewrite costs the same as a write: the head simply resets the bits.

2.7Limited lifetime and wear leveling

Flash wears out: the strong electric field that sets and resets bits eventually breaks down the silicon. The classic figures are 100,000 write-erase cycles for NOR and 1,000,000 for NAND. After that a cell is unreliable, and eventually a device can no longer save data.

Wear leveling spreads writes and erases over the whole chip: keep a count of writes in each page's trailer and pick the free page with the lowest count; scatter data freely, since distant pages are as fast as nearby ones. Most flash device controllers now do this in hardware.

Try itKeep rewriting one small file

Toy endurance: 100 erases per sector instead of 100,000, so the difference shows in a few hundred rewrites.

Modern endurance

The 1,000,000-cycle NAND figure is from single-level cells. The multi-level (MLC) and triple-level (TLC) cells in today's SSDs store 2–3 bits per cell and last roughly 1,000–10,000 cycles, which is why wear leveling is no longer optional.

2.8Why disk file systems hurt flash

  1. Fixed locations. A disk file system keeps its directory structure, file headers, allocation structures (the FAT), free-space bitmap, and often file data at fixed locations. Modifying them over and over at the same spots on flash quickly exhausts those locations, and then the whole chip. That is the "fixed location" run in §2.7.
  2. Rewrites treated as cheap. On a disk a rewrite costs the same as a write, and rapid rewrites are fast because there is little seeking. On flash a rewrite is far slower than a write to a clean page; rapid rewrites would mean disastrous latency and a shorter lifetime.

2.9Flash file systems

A flash file system must handle both problems: rewrites need erases, so hold writes back; and lifetime is limited.

  • Log-structured file systems fit flash well. Every write, and every rewrite, is appended to the end of a log; the log is the file system. The log grows sequentially and never rewrites a location in place, so there is less erasing and a longer lifetime.
  • JFFS and JFFS2 (Journaling Flash File System) work this way. Log entries hold everything needed to reconstruct the files, much like the log-based recovery of Lecture 20. The log can be circular, wrapping around once all free pages are used: free space is the distance between its head and its tail, and as it shrinks, garbage collection reclaims stale pages.

Chapter 3

RAID: redundant arrays of inexpensive disks

Disks are cheap. Attach several, and trade capacity for speed, for safety, or for both.

3.1Why an array of disks?

  • Faster access: reads and writes proceed on several disks in parallel. Data is striped across disks, for example each byte of an 8-byte word on a different disk.
  • Better reliability: if one disk fails, a copy of its data, or enough information to rebuild it, is on another disk. Redundancy to the rescue.

3.2RAID levels

LevelIdeaWhat you get
RAID 0striping, no redundancyfast parallel reads and writes; one failure loses data
RAID 1mirror each diskredundancy, but not RAID 0's speedup. A read can go to all mirrors and take the first answer; a synchronous write takes twice as long, which the OS can hide by delaying writes
RAID 0+1stripe, then mirror the stripespeed + redundancy; if a disk fails in both stripe sets, the data is lost
RAID 1+0 (10)mirror each disk, then stripeany two disks may fail, unless they mirror the same data: more robust than 0+1
RAID 2error-correcting code (ECC) bits on other disksmore compact than full copies, with strong guarantees
RAID 3bit-interleaved parity on one parity diskonly one extra disk; parity is computed in hardware so writes stay fast
RAID 4block-interleaved parity on one parity diskone extra disk; every write also updates the parity disk
RAID 5block-interleaved distributed parityparity spread over all disks, so no parity-disk bottleneck: the most common parity RAID
RAID 6RAID 5 with Reed-Solomon codes instead of paritysurvives two simultaneous disk failures

3.4How parity rebuilds a disk

For each position, the parity bit is the XOR of the data bits at that position on the other disks: P = D0 ⊕ D1 ⊕ D2.

XOR is its own inverse. If disk 1 dies, recompute its bit from everything that survived: D1 = D0 ⊕ D2 ⊕ P.

One parity disk therefore protects against any one failure, at the cost of one disk of capacity instead of half the array.

3.5Where RAID is implemented

SoftwarePlug disks into the bus; RAID management runs in the OS kernel, above the device drivers. Cheap and easy, but less efficient. On Linux, mdadm manages arrays.
Firmware / driverStandard disk-controller chips with RAID firmware and drivers recognize the disks and build the array. The system can boot from it, but the CPU still does part of the work.
HardwareA stand-alone array with its own RAID controller that looks like one physical device on the bus. The file system need not know about RAID. Expensive, highly efficient.

Epilogue

Summary and glossary

∎What to take away

  • Magnetic disks: access time = seek + rotational latency + transfer, dominated by the mechanical first two.
  • Disk scheduling reorders the request queue to cut seeks. FCFS is simple and slow; SSTF is greedy and can starve; SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK and C-LOOK sweep, trading a little distance for fairness. SSTF or LOOK are reasonable defaults.
  • Flash: no seeks, but a rewrite needs a sector-wide erase and every cell wears out. Wear leveling spreads erases; log-structured file systems avoid rewriting in place.
  • RAID: striping for speed, mirroring or parity for reliability. 1+0 beats 0+1 on failures; RAID 5 spreads parity to avoid RAID 4's bottleneck.
