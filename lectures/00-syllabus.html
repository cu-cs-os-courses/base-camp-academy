Chapter 0

Why study operating systems now

An LLM can do almost everything, but not everything.

0.1The honest question

Ask a model for "a C program that starts a child process and waits for it," and it returns correct, commented code faster than you can type the prompt. You may wonder why we spend a semester on what the kernel does underneath.

There are two reasons.

  1. An LLM cannot do everything. Only an expert can ask the right question, and only an expert can tell whether the answer is right.
  2. LLMs make computer systems more important, not less. Many core ideas in AI infrastructure come directly from classic computer systems, including the ones taught in this course.

0.2Reason 1: the right question, and the right check

In September 2026, OpenAI announced that its AI system had resolved a Millennium Prize Problem. Specifically, it showed that the 3D Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow can blow up in finite time. Note who did what.

Mathematicians · the questionChose the problem and the approach. The approach built on human work: the "infinite cascade" constructions of Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa, which use no computers at all.
AI agents · the searchUnder the researchers' direction, about 10,000 agents ran for roughly 88 hours and produced the proof.
Mathematicians · the checkThe proof was checked formally in Lean. However, humans still have to confirm that the statement Lean proved is the problem they set out to solve.
The lesson

The model did the work that nobody could do by hand. However, it did not choose the question, and it could not certify its own answer. Experts did both. The same holds for operating systems: the model writes the code, and you decide what to ask for and whether to trust what comes back.

Sources: Quanta Magazine, "AI Has Solved One of Math's $1 Million Millennium Prize Problems," Sept 8, 2026; OpenAI, "On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem."

0.3Asking the right question about a system

When you hand a broken program to an agent, what comes back depends on what you know to ask for.

  • "It's slow" gets guesses. "It spends 90% of its time blocked in read; is it the disk or the pipe?" gets an answer you can verify.
  • "It sometimes crashes" gets a rewrite. "Two threads update this list without a lock" gets a fix.
  • "It ran out of memory" gets a bigger machine. "Resident memory grows by one page per request and never shrinks" gets the leak found.
The course in one line

Learn to observe, model, and reason about running systems. As such, you can direct an LLM at systems problems and treat its output as a hypothesis to verify, not an answer to trust.

0.5Reason 2: LLMs make systems more important

vLLM, the paper's own words

"We propose PagedAttention, an attention algorithm inspired by the classical virtual memory and paging techniques in operating systems." Kwon et al., Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention, SOSP 2023.

The pattern

The ideas are fifty years old, and the hardware is new. Researchers who knew virtual memory recognized that GPU memory had the same problem, and they solved it first.

Chapter 1

What you will learn

Eight parts, twenty-four lectures, six labs, one running Linux kernel.

1.1Will I be able to write an OS?

  • In principle, yes. This course covers every important part of a simple operating system: processes, scheduling, synchronization, memory, devices, files, and protection.
  • In practice, slides and a textbook alone are not enough. Writing an OS requires reading hardware manuals and Linux source code, and writing and debugging a large amount of code.
  • Therefore, the labs do not ask you to build an OS from scratch. Instead, they put you inside a real one, Linux 6.6, and ask you to observe it, predict its behavior, and build small pieces that run on it: a process tree walker, a bounded buffer, a page-replacement policy, and a device driver.
Textbook

Silberschatz, Galvin, and Gagne, Operating System Concepts, 10th edition. Each lecture lists its chapters. Arpaci-Dusseau, Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (free online), is a good second reading. The Linux source is browsable at elixir.bootlin.com.

1.3Six Basecamps

A basecamp is where climbers build their skills before the real climb. In this course, each lab is a camp. All labs run in the same course VM on your own laptop: QEMU, a pinned Linux 6.6 kernel, and a Debian root file system. As such, the VM behaves identically on every machine, including yours and the graders'.

Lab00 · Part ILearning to See a Running SystemBuild the VM, then read the kernel's own view of processes, memory, and devices from /proc.
Lab01 · Part IIThe Life and Times of a ProcessWalk a process's ancestry, create children with fork, exec, and wait, and create zombies and orphans on purpose.
Lab02 · Part IIISharing the CPUMeasure how the Linux scheduler divides the CPU by nice value, and what a real-time policy does to all other processes.
Lab03 · Part IVThe Trouble with ThreadsMake a race condition visible, then build a bounded buffer that is correct under any interleaving.
Lab04 · Part VPages, Faults, and the Art of EvictionObserve demand paging, then write a page-replacement policy and tune it against a simulator.
Lab05 · Part VIEverything Is a FileDrive files with raw system calls, then write a character device driver, which is your own code running inside the kernel.

You check your own work with make check LAB=NN-name. Grading re-runs the same checks. Therefore, a local pass is a grading pass.

A fixed weekly rhythm, clear grading, and as little AI as possible.

2.3AI in this course: as little as possible

Chapter 0 argued that an AI is only as good as the person who directs it and checks its work. This course is where you become that person. Therefore, please use AI as little as you can here.

  • You cannot check what you never learned. If a model explains fork() to you, you cannot tell a correct explanation from a fluent but wrong one. Build the mental model yourself first.
  • Struggle is part of learning. Spending an evening stuck on a race condition and then finding it is exactly the experience that makes you fast later. A model that removes the struggle also removes the lesson.
  • Your submission must be your own. Nothing under a lab's submit/ directory, neither the code nor the report, may come from a model. Quizzes and exams are your own work alone.
When you are stuck, go here first

The lecture notes and the textbook chapter, the man page (man 2 fork), the Linux source on elixir.bootlin.com, Friday recitation, office hours, and Piazza.

Later: CSCI 4573

Once you have mastered the foundations, you can use AI as much as you like. In CSCI 4573, you will work with AI agents freely, as the expert who directs them and checks their output.

2.5Time, habits, and help

  • This course takes time. Four credits means about 4 hours per week in class and 8 to 12 hours outside of class, and more in some weeks.
  • Start each lab the week it opens. Waiting until the last weekend is the most reliable way to fail a lab. Debugging is the slow part, and it does not compress.
  • Work in small steps. Write a little code, run make check, and commit. Writing everything first and debugging it all at once is risky. Be prepared to discard code and start over.
  • Be an active learner. Ask questions in lecture and recitation. Passive learning leads to disengagement.
