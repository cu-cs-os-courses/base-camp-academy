Translate three addresses

16-bit logical addresses, 2 KB (211-byte) pages: 11 offset bits, 5 page-number bits.

page01234
frame52invalid70

Two ways to split an address:

  • Arithmetic: page = address ÷ 2048, offset = address mod 2048.
  • Bits: write it in binary and cut 11 bits from the right. With 2 KB pages the cut falls inside a hex digit.

Translate 0x0A3F, 0x1C05 and 0x1234.

Answers
logicaldecimalpage, offsetframephysical
0x0A3F26231, 575 (0x23F)22 × 2048 + 575 = 4671 = 0x123F
0x1C0571733, 1029 (0x405)77 << 11 = 0x3800; + 0x405 = 0x3C05
0x123446602, 564—invalid → trap

For 0x1234 the OS decides (Lecture 15). If page 2 belongs to the address space but is on disk, this is a page fault, and the OS loads the page. If it does not belong, the access is illegal and the process gets a segmentation fault.

Part 2 · follow along

Why two levels

32-bit addresses, 4 KB pages, 4-byte page-table entries.

  1. A flat table has 220 entries × 4 B = 4 MB per process, even for a tiny program.
  2. Split the address 10 | 10 | 12. The outer table has 1024 entries: 4 KB. Each inner table also has 1024 entries, 4 KB, and maps 1024 pages, 4 MB of address space. Inner tables exist only where memory is used.

A process with 8 MB of code and data at the bottom and a 4 MB stack at the top: how much page table? And how does 0x00805123 split?

Answers

2 + 1 inner tables plus the outer one: 4 KB + 3 × 4 KB = 16 KB, instead of 4 MB.

0x00805123: outer index = top 10 bits = 2, inner index = next 10 bits = 5, offset = 0x123.

The cost: without a TLB, one translation now takes two table reads before the data read. x86-64 uses four levels.

Part 3 · follow along

Effective access time

Memory access 50 ns, TLB lookup negligible, TLB hit ratio 95%.

Compute the EAT for a one-level table, then a two-level table, then two levels plus demand paging with 1 fault per million accesses at 5 ms each.

Answers
  1. One level: a hit is 1 access, a miss is 2. EAT = 0.95 × 50 + 0.05 × 100 = 52.5 ns.
  2. Two levels: a miss is 3 accesses. EAT = 0.95 × 50 + 0.05 × 150 = 55 ns.
  3. With faults: EAT ≈ (1 − 10−6) × 55 + 10−6 × 5,000,000 ≈ 55 + 5 = 60 ns.

One fault in a million accesses costs as much as all the TLB misses together. That is why the fault rate must be tiny, and why replacement policy (Lecture 16, after the break) matters. Try other values in the Lecture 14 and Lecture 15 calculators.
